\section{What the experiments establish}
\label{sec:experiments}
The work formulas tell us what each method must do. Measurements answer the next question:
does the scoring it avoids pay for the search it adds? We first compare real embeddings,
then use constructed inputs to isolate the importance of the query's strong coordinates.
The saved source record identifies the runs behind these comparisons~\cite{local}.

\subsection{Make the comparison fair}
A, B and C receive the same documents and floating-point queries. They use the same native
weighted binary scorer, return the top 100, and break score ties by document ID. Recall is
recovery of that exact binary ranking. There is no float reranker or human relevance judgment
in these comparisons.

The machine is an Apple M5 Max with 128 GiB of physical memory. Each worker uses one CPU
thread and runs one query at a time, under a separate 32 GB decimal memory budget. A fresh
process builds a configuration, warms it, and measures routing plus search. This outer timer
includes the call from Python into C++. Cached-vector measurements exclude text encoding.
Repeated process blocks help expose timing variation; the recorded power settings do not
prove that CPU frequency remained fixed.

We tune parameters on one set of query IDs, freeze the choices, and evaluate them on different
IDs. Each method may choose its own fastest tested setting that meets the recall requirement.
A gets intermediate probe counts and direct routing by sign keys. B gets large leaves, so it
can score a selected cluster without making unnecessary splits. Without these options, a
poor baseline or a forced traversal could decide the result before the timing begins.

The real study retains two selection rules. One accepts settings whose average tuning recall
reaches the target. The conservative rule requires a lower estimate from repeated resampling
of tuning queries to reach it. This leaves more margin, but cannot promise recall on future
queries. A supported speedup also requires both methods to meet the target and the paired
95\% interval for their time ratio to lie above one. Queries and whole process blocks are
resampled together; timing repeats do not count as additional independent queries.

\subsection{Real embeddings did not establish an alternative speedup}
The real study uses 256-bit document codes from Nomic embeddings of MS MARCO passages~\cite{nomic,msmarco}.
After combining 6,821 tuning settings, the frozen choices were evaluated on 200 query IDs
excluded from the earlier 300-query collection. All 36 conservative target points met their
requested recall on this sample; only 7 of 36 points chosen by average tuning recall did.

The table shows the conservative results at one million documents. Read each row as a common
minimum recall requirement, followed by each method's median routing-plus-search time.
The achieved recall happens to be equal across methods in these rows.

None of the 48 evaluated comparisons across the real study established a B or C speedup
under the stated rule. The conclusion applies to this data, score, implementation and tested
parameter range. Fewer fully scored documents did not produce a supported reduction in the
complete retrieval time.

A later control gives C a one-bit key, A's routing layout, and unlimited candidate and lookup
budgets. A and B keep their frozen settings. It uses 100 further query IDs, excluding all
500 previously used IDs. A, B and C measured 9.541, 9.855 and 10.545 ms, respectively.
All three achieved 98.95\% recall, below the requested 99\%. We retain that miss without
rounding it into a pass. It is a separate sample from the earlier successful 200-query
evaluation, and no parameter was changed in response to its outcome.

\subsection{Constructed data shows when skipping scores can help}
The controlled inputs make a specific distinction testable. Document signs are independent
and balanced across 256 coordinates. Each query has 13 strong coordinates of magnitude one;
the remaining coordinates have magnitude 0.0001. Matching every strong sign therefore beats
any document that mismatches one, regardless of the weak signs. In the fixed case, the strong
positions stay the same. In the changing case, each query chooses different strong positions
uniformly without replacement. These are constructed vectors, not measured text embeddings.

The final follow-up uses 40 tuning queries and 20 separate evaluation queries, with three
process blocks and three timing repeats. It adds neighboring key widths, cluster counts and
leaf sizes, then remeasures earlier choices. The results below use one million documents and
the 99\% requirement. All methods reach 100\% recall except A in the changing case, at 99.95\%.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Strong positions & A (ms) & B (ms) & C (ms)\\
\midrule
Fixed & 0.0140 & 0.0160 & 0.0128\\
Changing & 24.9235 & 0.1446 & 14.6301\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With fixed positions, A selects two of 16,384 clusters by direct routing; C uses one physical
cluster and a 13-bit key. C's paired speedup estimate is 1.098, with a 95\% interval of
1.066--1.147. The time difference is about 1.25 microseconds. Both methods select documents
through the same strong signs. C combines key enumeration and scoring in one native call;
A routes and scans through separate calls. The small measured advantage concerns this
implementation of closely related candidate selection.

Changing positions prevent a fixed key from consistently selecting the strong signs. B can
follow them because it orders coordinates using each query's magnitudes. Its selected setting
uses one cluster, unlimited traversal and a leaf size of 160. A opens 255 of 256 centroid
clusters. B's paired speedup estimate is 172.3, with a 95\% interval of 169.1--177.2.
C uses a 14-bit key, but its paired interval against A includes one; its lower median alone
does not establish an advantage.

The leaf choice follows the occupancy calculation. At one million rows, the ideal 13-bit
group has mean size about 122 and standard deviation about 11. A threshold of 128 can force
another split on a weak coordinate. A threshold of 160 leaves more room to score that group.
The experiment checks this distribution-based suggestion. It does not establish that 160
is best for arbitrary embeddings, sizes or machines.

\subsection{Check the answer, the work, then the predicted time}
An independent answer comes first. The checks calculate scores directly from document signs
and sort by score and ID. Larger runs count strong and weak mismatches from packed bits,
avoiding a huge expanded matrix. Every complete native call in the scaling check returned
the independent reference ranking.

Next, predicted work is compared with native counters. Across the earlier controlled tuning
and evaluation runs, 447 applicable per-query predictions matched exactly. These checks
include full-width split and leaf visits, scored rows, key attempts including empty lookups,
and tracked mask peaks where applicable. Queries outside the formulas' stated conditions
remain in the measurements; they are not counted as successful predictions.

Correct counts do not give every row the same time cost. In a 16-million-document buffer,
scoring about 1,958 scattered rows took 0.159 ms with reused candidates and 0.455 ms with
changing candidates. Candidate generation was outside the timer, but changed which memory
had been accessed recently. Fits trained on one- and four-million-row buffers underestimated
a 16-million-row check by as much as 45.2\% for reused candidates and 27.6\% for changing
candidates. Both failed fits remain saved. The largest row store was about 640 MB, so these
checks do not validate access costs for a 40 GB store.

The later complete-call model uses one latency median per corpus size. This corrects an
earlier fit that gave fast individual queries too much weight when predicting a median.
For C's small postings, the revised model also accounts for result-selection work. B's leaf
threshold grows as $L=160N/10^6$, keeping the intended strong-coordinate depth instead of
forcing more cuts as the corpus grows.

The revised four-million-row check had already been inspected, so it is diagnostic. The
coefficients were frozen before the separate eight-million-row run below. Positive error
means the prediction was slower than the measurement.

All six checks met the earlier 20\% criterion. They test transfer to a larger corpus under
the same prescribed workload and existing query IDs. These are model-check configurations,
not the tuned comparisons in the preceding table. The largest observed error, 7.7\%, is not
a guaranteed bound for future queries or a billion documents.

\subsection{Measure actual text requests}
A user supplies text, so retrieval time covers only part of the wait. The final check runs
20 existing independent-query texts through the cached local Nomic model on MPS, normalizes
each vector, routes it and calls native search. Batch size is one. Three blocks and two
repeats give 120 requests per method, or 360 requests in total. Each sequence shape is warmed;
model loading, index building and reference creation are outside the request timer.

The table reports stage medians and the separately measured request total. All three methods
use the earlier frozen settings and achieve 99.45\% binary recall on this sample.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & Encode (ms) & Retrieve (ms) & Total (ms) & Total p95 (ms)\\
\midrule
A & 6.488 & 9.406 & 16.313 & 19.201\\
B & 6.500 & 9.651 & 16.303 & 19.217\\
C & 7.543 & 11.201 & 18.912 & 21.898\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The median is the middle time; p95 is the 95th-percentile time. The tiny A/B total difference
does not establish a speedup. Adding stage medians need not reproduce the total median:
the request with the middle encoding time need not have the middle retrieval time. Each
request's total must be measured before taking its median.

These timings apply to real Nomic text queries. The constructed strong-coordinate vectors
do not come from this encoder. Adding its time to their search measurements would not create
a measured text-to-results pipeline.
